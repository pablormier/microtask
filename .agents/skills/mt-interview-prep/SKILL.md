# Interview prep

Help interviewers with little formal training run a useful, fair, and human
conversation. Apply structured-interviewing principles with judgement, not a
fixed template. The goal is not more questions but relevant evidence within the
time available. The defaults assume an academic or research setting (a lab,
a project, a PI or staff interviewer); adapt them when the role is different.

Follow the [microtask skill](../microtask/SKILL.md) for all card operations.

## When a card is about an interview

When the user creates or triages a card about interviewing someone, offer once
to add a preparation section; do not add one unprompted, and drop it if the
user declines. If they accept, gather what is missing, without asking again for
what the card or conversation already holds:

- **Position:** role, career stage, and the skills that actually matter.
- **Purpose:** first conversation, screening, technical or research discussion,
  or final selection; duration; who else interviews.
- **Candidate:** CV, motivation letter, research interests, and how they reached
  the lab (emails, referral, advert).

Ask in one short message, and proceed with what you have if the user prefers.

Candidate material is personal data, and a workspace may sync to a shared Git
remote. Ask before attaching a CV or quoting correspondence into a card; a short
summary with a pointer to where the file lives is often enough. Delete nothing
about the candidate without permission.

Save agreed prep with `mt edit REF --body --append --file -`. Add interview
notes and the debrief to the same card later, keeping observations separate
from the decision.

## Design the interview

Choose a structure that fits the purpose and time. Candidate areas include
motivation and interest in the lab, past work and the candidate's own
contribution, technical knowledge, scientific reasoning, learning and
independence, collaboration and expectations, and the candidate's questions.
Pick only what this interview needs: a short slot should cover fewer areas well
rather than many superficially. Leave time for follow-ups and the candidate's
questions.

Calibrate to career stage: an intern is assessed on curiosity and learning, a
postdoc on independence and research ability. Briefly say why the structure fits
and what it cannot reliably assess.

## Write questions

- Short, plain, one thing at a time; easy for non-native English speakers.
  Technical terms only where the role needs them.
- Prefer concrete past experience and realistic situations over hypotheticals
  that invite rehearsed answers.
- Add a neutral follow-up where it would sharpen the evidence.
- Remind the interviewer to let candidates ask for clarification and take time
  to think. Accent, vocabulary, confidence, and speaking speed are not
  competence.

Personalise from the candidate's materials (a CV project, a stated interest, a
tool they used), and mark what is documented versus assumed. When several
candidates compete for one post, keep the same core areas and standards even if
examples differ. Do not reward prestige, networks, familiarity with academic
culture, or similarity to the interviewer; separate demonstrated skill and real
alignment from polish.

## Evaluate fairly

When it helps, give each section a goal and the evidence to look for. For formal
selection, add simple scoring anchors and allow "insufficient evidence" instead
of forcing a judgement. Encourage notes on what was said before impressions, and
scoring before discussing with other panel members. No single answer, first
impression, or vague "fit" should decide.

## Challenge poor practice

Do not accept every requested question or criterion. If one is discriminatory,
intrusive, misleading, unrelated to the role, or likely to bias the result, say
so plainly, explain the effect on fairness or evidence quality, and offer an
alternative that serves the user's real goal. For example, a question about
family plans to check commitment becomes a question about the position's actual
availability requirements, asked of every candidate.

Common problems: "culture fit" or similarity as evidence; polished English as
proof of research ability; publication demands for a learning-focused
internship; questions about protected characteristics or private life; trick
questions or unrealistic tasks without a purpose; strong conclusions from thin
evidence.

Be proportionate. A harmless informal question needs no warning, and
institutional requirements or reasonable preferences deserve respect.

## Output

Produce the smallest result that serves the request:

- **Compact card:** agenda with timings, a goal per section, and one or two
  questions each.
- **Guide:** adds follow-ups and what to listen for.
- **Full kit** (when asked): competencies, questions, scoring rubric, note
  space, and a debrief format.

Size follows the request, not the interview length. For each section give time,
goal, and questions. Skip generic advice and long introductions; vary the shape
to fit the interview rather than repeating one template.
